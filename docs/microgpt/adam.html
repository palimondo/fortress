7.1 The plain rule first

The gradient says, for each parameter, which way the loss goes when the parameter goes up. Training moves each parameter the other way. The plainest rule takes the same small fraction of every gradient entry:

  • p is the vector of parameters, g the gradient, and p' the new parameters.

  • lrlr is a small positive number, the learning rate.

The weakness of this rule is that gradient entries differ widely in size. In the step on yuheng, parameter 500 has the entry -0.0926. The stored gradient that the quick check of Part 8 compares with gives parameter 1000 the entry -0.00000079, a hundred thousand times smaller. With one learning rate for both, either the first parameter moves too far or the second hardly moves.

The program uses a rule that sets the size of the move for each parameter by itself. Its name is Adam, short for adaptive moment estimation. Karpathy's microGPT uses it too. Adam keeps two more numbers for every parameter, from step to step: an average of the parameter's recent gradient entries, and an average of their squares.

7.2 Adam

Adam's constants are in line 18 of MicroGptFlat.fss:

18 
18(epsilon, lr0, beta1, beta2, epsilon_A, nSteps) = (10.0^(-5), 0.01, 0.85, 0.99, 10.0^(-8), 1000)
18(ε, lr0, β1, β2, ε_A, nSteps) = (10.0^(-5), 0.01, 0.85, 0.99, 10.0^(-8), 1000)

  • β1beta1β1 is 0.85 and β2beta2β2 is 0.99. They are the shares of the old value that the two averages keep at each step.

  • ϵAepsilon_Aε_A, Adam's epsilon, is 10.0−810.0^(-8), which is 0.00000001. Adam adds it to a number before it divides by that number.

  • lr0lr0 is 0.01, the learning rate at step 0. nStepsnSteps is 1,000, the number of steps over which the learning rate falls to 0.

  • ϵepsilonε, the first name, is not Adam's. It is the 0.00001 of rmsnrmsn in Part 3.

Lines 75 and 76 are the header. It is long, so the result type stands on a line of its own.

  • pp is the vector of parameters, and gg the gradient that stepstep returned.

  • mm and vv are the two vectors of averages. Each has 4,192 numbers, like pp. Both start as zeros.

  • tt is the number of the step, counted from 1. lrlr is the learning rate for this step.

  • The result is three vectors: the new pp, the new mm, the new vv.

A name may end in a prime: m'm' is a name of its own, read "m prime". Here it is the new mm.

Line 77 is the average of the gradient. β1beta1β1 is 0.85. The new average is 85 parts of the old average and 15 parts of the new gradient. β1 mbeta1 mβ1 m is a number beside a vector: juxtaposition multiplies every element. ++ adds two vectors element by element.

Line 78 is the average of the squares. g × gg × g multiplies the gradient with itself, element by element. β2beta2β2 is 0.99: 99 parts old, 1 part new.

Line 79 is the move. It has three pieces.

  • m' / (1 − β1t)m' / (1 - beta1^t)m' / (1 - β1^t) corrects the first average for its start. mm starts as 0, so after the first step m'm' is only 15 parts in 100 of the gradient. At tt = 1 the divisor is 1 minus 0.85, which is 0.15, and the division gives the whole gradient back. At tt = 3 the divisor is 0.386. As tt grows, the divisor goes to 1, and the correction changes less and less.

  • v' / (1 − β2t)v' / (1 - beta2^t)v' / (1 - β2^t) is the same correction for the second average. √SQRT√ takes the root of every element. + ϵA+ epsilon_A+ ε_A adds 0.00000001 to every element. A root is 0 or more, so every element is then at least 0.00000001, and the division that follows never divides by 0.

  • The first piece times lrlr, divided by the second piece, element by element, is the move. Line 79 subtracts it from pp.

Line 80 is the block's value, the triple. The comment in line 74 says "three whole-vector expressions": the function has no loop and no index. It changes none of its arguments.

  • β1 is β1beta1β1, β2 is β2beta2β2, and εA is ϵAepsilon_Aε_A.

  • g ⊙ g is g × gg × g: the product element by element.

  • The root, the division and the powers apply to every element by itself.

The numbers of parameter 500 in the first step show what the three lines do. A run printed its value before, its gradient entry, its two averages after the step, its value after, and the difference:

index 500 p0 0.06258901428847981 g -0.09262792010673876 m1 -0.013894188016010816 v1 8.579931583300387E-5 p1 0.07258901320889183 delta 0.00999999892041202

  • m1 is 0.15 times the gradient entry: -0.01389.

  • v1 is 0.01 times the entry's square: 0.0000858.

  • The corrected first average is m1 divided by 0.15: the entry itself, -0.0926.

  • The corrected second average is v1 divided by 0.01: the entry's square. Its root is 0.0926, the entry without its sign.

  • Their quotient is -1. The move is lrlr times -1, and lrlr is 0.01 in the first step. Subtracting it raises the parameter by 0.01: from 0.0626 to 0.0726.

So in the first step, the size of the entry cancels out. Only its sign is left. Two more parameters from the same run:

index 1100 p0 0.03654080103626847 g 0.031736131393025356 m1 0.004760419708953804 v1 1.0071820357953705E-5 p1 0.026540804187250147 delta -0.009999996849018325

  • Parameter 1100 has a positive entry, a third the size of the first one. It moves down, also by 0.01.

  • Parameter 0 lies in the row of wtewte for the token a. Its entry is 0, and it does not move: 0 divided by ϵAepsilon_Aε_A is 0.

In later steps the averages hold several gradients. A parameter whose entries keep one sign goes on moving by about lrlr in each step. A parameter whose entries change sign has a small first average and a large second one, and it moves less.

The parentheses in line 79 fix the order of the float operations: first lrlr times the corrected average, then the division. The order is that of the reference program in NumPy, whose values the quick check of Part 8 compares with.

7.3 The learning rate

82 
82learningRate(s: ZZ32): RR64 = lr0 (1 - (1.0 s) / nSteps)
82learningRate(s: ZZ32): RR64 = lr0 (1 - (1.0 s) / nSteps)

The learning rate falls in a straight line from lr0lr0, 0.01, at step 0, towards 0 at step nStepsnSteps, 1,000. A run printed three values:

lr(0) = 0.01 lr(1) = 0.00999 lr(999) = 1.000000000000001E-5

lr0 (…)lr0 (...) is a number beside a number: the product. 1.0 s1.0 s turns the integer ss into a float. The line needs it. In Fortress, // between two integers gives an exact fraction, a value of the type ℚQQ: for ss = 3, a run printed s / nStepss / nSteps as 3/1000. The library has no product of a float and such a fraction, and the line without 1.01.0 stops with "Failed to find any matching overload". With the float on the left, the division gives a float.

The schedule is made for 1,000 steps, the number that the reference program of Part 8 was run with. The loop below runs five.

A limit of today's tree

1.0 s1.0 s runs under walk. The compiled path's checker refuses it today when it checks against the interpreter's library: it finds two products that fit only after a conversion, of two floats and of two fractions, and cannot choose (the gap ledger's row 989).

7.5 What one step does to the numbers

To see the effect of a step, measure the same name before and after. A run did the step on yuheng, applied Adam once, and called stepstep on yuheng again with the new parameters:

loss doc 0 before 3.365966947584851 after one Adam step 2.9510500702232525

The loss on yuheng fell from 3.366 to 2.951. In probabilities, a run printed expexp of minus each loss:

exp(-3.365966947584851) = 0.034528612612313464  exp(-2.9510500702232525) = 0.052284774427746754

These are the probabilities that give the same two losses if all seven right answers have one and the same probability: 0.035 before the step, 0.052 after it.

The same change helped a little on a name that the step did not see. The loss on document 1, diondre, with the old and with the new parameters:

loss doc 1 with p0 3.4265763956879174 with p1 3.4242727838717717

The second number is the loss that the program printed for step 2.

The whole step on yuheng, in numbers:

  1. The keys. The name becomes 16 rows of tokens, of which 7 count: idsids, tgtg, vmvm, pospos.

  2. The forward pass. From the keys and the 4,192 parameters it computes a matrix of 16 by 27 probabilities. The seven right answers have probabilities between 0.027 and 0.046. The loss is 3.366.

  3. The backward pass. It computes 4,192 rates, one for each parameter. 464 of them are 0: the parameters that this name did not use.

  4. Adam. Each of the other 3,728 parameters moves against the sign of its rate. In the first step the move is 0.01, or a little less where the rate is within a few powers of ten of ϵAepsilon_Aε_A.

  5. With the new parameters, the loss on the same name is 2.951.

7.6 What the program leaves out

  • It never makes a name. Karpathy's program ends with a loop that does: it gives the model the mark, makes probabilities from the scores of that position, draws a token by them, appends the token, and repeats until the mark is drawn or the block's positions are used. The flat program stops after training. Its prpr holds probabilities of the kind that such a loop draws from.

  • It runs five steps, where the learning rate's schedule is made for 1,000. The five steps are enough to compare the program's numbers with the stored values, which is the program's use today.

  • Its batch is one document. The step itself takes a batch of any size: every matrix then has 16 rows for each document, and the planes of attention keep the documents apart.
