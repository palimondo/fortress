Part 3 Two functions on a row: rmsn and sm

Two small functions that the model calls again and again: one brings a row of numbers to a standard size, the other turns a row of scores into probabilities. Each is shown first on two or three numbers, then on a real row.

3.1 rmsn: bring a row to a standard size

26 27 
26(* -------------------------------------- rmsn, sm and their backward ------ *)27rmsn(x: Array[\RR64,ZZ32\]): Array[\RR64,ZZ32\] = x / SQRT (epsilon + (x DOT x) / |x|)
26(* -------------------------------------- rmsn, sm and their backward ------ *)27rmsn(x: Array⟦RR64,ZZ32⟧): Array⟦RR64,ZZ32⟧ = x / √ (ε + (x · x) / |x|)

Line 26 is the heading comment for lines 27 to 34. Lines 30 to 34 hold two more functions, rmsn_brmsn_b and sm_bsm_b, with which the model computes its gradient. Part 6 reads them.

Line 27 declares a function. The form is name(parameter: Type): ResultType = expressionname(parameter: Type): ResultType = expression. This one takes a vector of floats and returns a vector of floats. rmsnrmsn is short for RMS norm, and RMS for root mean square: the function divides a vector by the root of the mean of its squares.

The line uses one constant, ϵepsilonε. Line 18 of MicroGptFlat.fss sets it, as the first name of six, to 10.0−510.0^(-5), which is 0.00001:

18 
18(epsilon, lr0, beta1, beta2, epsilon_A, nSteps) = (10.0^(-5), 0.01, 0.85, 0.99, 10.0^(-8), 1000)
18(ε, lr0, β1, β2, ε_A, nSteps) = (10.0^(-5), 0.01, 0.85, 0.99, 10.0^(-8), 1000)

Read the right side of line 27 from the inside out, with the vector 3, 4 as xx:

  • x · xx DOT xx · x is the dot product of xx with itself: 3 times 3, plus 4 times 4, is 25. It is the sum of the squares.

  • |x||x| is the number of elements of xx: 2. The two bars are an operator that encloses its operand. On an array it gives the size.

  • (x · x) / |x|(x DOT x) / |x|(x · x) / |x| is 12.5: the mean of the squares.

  • ϵ + …epsilon + ...ε + ... adds 0.00001 to the mean. The reason is the division at the end. For a vector of zeros the mean is 0, its root is 0, and the function would divide by 0. A sum of squares cannot be negative, so the mean plus ϵepsilonε is at least 0.00001 and can never be 0.

  • √SQRT√ is the square root, written as a prefix operator. The root of 12.50001 is 3.5355.

  • x / …x / ... divides every element of xx by that root. Line 27 of FlatArrays.fss, the file of array helpers that the program imports, declares // between an array and a number.

A run printed the result, and then the mean of the squares of the result:

rmsn [3 4] (2): 0.8485277980128058 1.1313703973504077
mean square of rmsn [3 4] = 0.9999992000006401

After the division, the mean square of the vector is 1, up to the small ϵepsilonε. The proportions of the elements stay: 0.8485 to 1.1314 is still 3 to 4.

The vector 30, 40 has the same proportions and ten times the size. Its result is almost the same:

rmsn [30 40] (2): 0.8485281340297446 1.1313708453729927

That is the function's job in the model: it removes the overall size of a row and keeps the proportions of its numbers. The model applies it at three places, so that the rows neither grow nor shrink from stage to stage.

  • x is the parameter xx, and xi is its element number i.

  • The sum under the root adds the squares of all the elements. It is x · xx DOT xx · x.

  • n is |x||x|, the number of elements.

  • ε is ϵepsilonε. Every square is 0 or more, so the sum is 0 or more, and the number under the root is at least 0.00001. The root is never 0.

The sum of the squares is a plain sum, and at this size it is safe. In the model, xx has 16 elements. The model gives three matrices to rmsnrmsn, row by row. Their names are xx, xpxp and x2x2. For each, a run printed the largest element, without its sign, and the largest sum of squares of a row, sumsq:

max |x| = 0.3009792670763558  max |xp| = 2.4877144323887146
max sumsq x = 0.3399218094145408  max sumsq xp = 15.992472399495856
max |x2| = 2.55589149111105  max sumsq x2 = 16.803895081622983

  • Overflow: an ℝ64RR64 holds numbers up to about 10 to the power 308. The largest sum here is 16.8. A square overflows only for an element above 10 to the power 154.

  • Accuracy: an ℝ64RR64 keeps about 16 digits, and each addition can change the last of them. The sum has 16 terms, all 0 or more and of similar size, so nothing cancels. The loss of the whole step agrees with the loss of the reference program in NumPy in all 16 digits, as Part 8 shows.

Welford's method computes a mean and a variance over a long stream of numbers in one pass, without keeping the numbers. It updates the mean with each new number, so it never subtracts one large sum from another, which is where the plain formula for a variance loses its digits. rmsnrmsn needs the mean of 16 squares and no variance. It subtracts nothing, and the plain sum is enough.

A limit of today's tree

The parameter's type is Array⟦ℝ64,ℤ32⟧Array[\RR64,ZZ32\]Array⟦RR64,ZZ32⟧, a vector of any length. The library's own type for a vector of floats is Vector⟦ℝ64,n⟧Vector[\RR64,n\]Vector⟦RR64,n⟧, with the length nn in the type. The model passes rmsnrmsn to another function, rowsrows, in line 57, as the next section shows. Under walk, a function whose type has a size parameter cannot be passed as an argument: the call fails (the gap ledger's row 156). So the four functions of lines 27 to 34 take the type without a size.

rowsrows is a function of FlatArrays.fss. It takes a function and a matrix. It applies the function to each row of the matrix and puts the results together as the rows of a new matrix. In Fortress a function is a value, so the first argument is the name rmsnrmsn and nothing more. Its definition:

  • Line 146 is the block's value: the filled matrix.

A small case, with the 3 by 2 matrix xx of Part 2, whose rows are 5, 6 and 1, 0 and 0, 1:

Each row has been divided by the root of its own mean square.

In the function stepstep, xx is the 16 by 16 matrix of Part 2: each row is a token's embedding plus a position's embedding. For its row 1, a run printed the mean square before and after:

meansq x[1] = 0.01127520082084148  meansq xp[1] = 0.9991138837351009  meansq x1[1] = 0.9999899912311551

  • The embeddings are small numbers: the mean square of row 1 of xx is 0.0113.

  • Row 1 of xpxp has the mean square 0.9991. It is not exactly 1, because ϵepsilonε is not tiny beside 0.0113.

  • x1x1 is rmsnrmsn applied a second time. It changes little: 0.9991 becomes 0.99999.

The model keeps both. The next stage is attention, in which a position reads earlier positions. Attention reads x1x1, and line 60 adds attention's result to xpxp. Karpathy's program has the same two calls, one after the other, and also adds attention's result to the first.

3.3 sm: scores into probabilities

28 
28sm(z: Array[\RR64,ZZ32\]): Array[\RR64,ZZ32\] = do e = exp(z - (BIG MAX z)); e / (SUM e) end
28sm(z: Array⟦RR64,ZZ32⟧): Array⟦RR64,ZZ32⟧ = do e = exp(z - (BIG MAX z)); e / (SUM e) end

smsm is short for softmax. It takes a vector of numbers of any size and sign, here called scores, and returns a vector of probabilities: every element is between 0 and 1, and the elements add up to 1. A higher score gets a higher probability.

The body is a block with two steps, separated by a semicolon. With the scores 1, 2, 3 as zz, a run printed each stage:

z - BIG MAX z (3): -2.0 -1.0 0.0
exp(z - BIG MAX z) (3): 0.1353352832366127 0.36787944117144233 1.0
sm z (3): 0.09003057317038046 0.24472847105479764 0.6652409557748218
SUM sm(z) = 0.9999999999999999

  • BIG MAX zBIG MAX z is the largest element of zz: 3. BIG MAXBIG MAX is a big operator like ∑SUM. Written before an array, it takes all the array's elements.

  • z − (BIG MAX z)z - (BIG MAX z) subtracts 3 from every element. The library declares −- between an array and a number.

  • expexp is the exponential function: exp(t)exp(t) is the number 2.718… raised to the power tt. exp(0)exp(0) is 1, exp(−1)exp(-1) is 0.368, exp(−2)exp(-2) is 0.135. Its result is always positive. FlatArrays.fss declares expexp for arrays: it applies expexp to every element.

  • ee is the name of that vector of positive numbers. ∑eSUM e is their sum, 1.5032.

  • e / (∑e)e / (SUM e) divides every element by the sum. Now the elements add up to 1.

  • z is the parameter zz, and zi its element number i.

  • maxj zj is BIG MAX zBIG MAX z.

  • e is the local variable ee.

  • ∑j ej is ∑eSUM e.

Four facts about smsm, each from a run.

A step of 1 in the score multiplies the probability by 2.718. The scores 1, 2, 3 are one apart, and 0.2447 is 2.718 times 0.0900.

Only the differences between the scores count. Adding 100 to every score gives the same probabilities:

sm(z + 100) (3): 0.09003057317038046 0.24472847105479764 0.6652409557748218

This is also why the program may subtract the maximum: it changes nothing in the result. It is there for the arithmetic. expexp of a large number does not fit into a float, and after the subtraction the largest number that expexp sees is 0. A run shows it on the scores 1001, 1002, 1003:

exp(1000.0) = Infinity
sm [1001 1002 1003] (3): 0.09003057317038046 0.24472847105479764 0.6652409557748218
exp z / SUM exp z (3): NaN NaN NaN

The last line is the same computation without the subtraction. It gives no numbers.

Larger differences make the result more uneven. With the scores doubled to 2, 4, 6, the largest gets 87 percent:

sm(2 z) (3): 0.015876239976466765 0.11731042782619838 0.8668133321973349

A score far below the others gets the probability 0, exactly. With a fourth score of minus 10 to the power 10:

sm [1 2 3 -1e10] (4): 0.09003057317038046 0.24472847105479764 0.6652409557748218 0.0

The other three probabilities are what they were without the fourth score. Attention uses this in Part 4: it gives such a score to every position that a row must not read.

The model calls smsm in two places. In line 59 it turns the scores of attention into attention weights, the numbers that say how much one position reads from another. In line 61 it turns the 27 scores of each position into the 27 probabilities that are the model's answer.
